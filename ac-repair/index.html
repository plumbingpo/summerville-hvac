A humming outdoor unit with a fan that will not turn is the call we get most in July in Summerville, SC, and the cause is usually a failed run capacitor. That inexpensive component starts the compressor and fan, fades for years without complaint, and then quits under peak load. It is far from the only suspect, though. Contactors pit and stop passing clean power, condenser fan bearings dry out, small refrigerant leaks drain away cooling capacity, and evaporator coils ice over when a clogged filter strangles airflow.

At Summerville HVAC, each cooling call starts with measurement. Capacitors are metered against the rating on the label. Amp draws on the compressor and fan motor are compared to the data plate. Refrigerant charge is judged by superheat and subcooling, not by pressure readings alone, and any icing prompts a look at the evaporator coil, blower wheel, and filter. This matters because one warm-air complaint can hide four different faults at four very different prices, and a refrigerant top-off that ignores the leak is just a short-term rental on cold air.

Straight answers are part of every visit. If an aging system's compressor has failed, we explain the repair-versus-replacement math plainly and let you decide. Most of the time, though, the fix is simpler, and because our trucks stock capacitors, contactors, fan motors, hard-start kits, and condensate pumps, cooling is usually restored before we leave. When to Call for AC Repair in Summerville

Catching these issues early helps you avoid costly problems and protect your property. These are the signals our technicians see most often when a problem is underway:

  • Water leaking around the indoor unit or from the ceiling below it
  • Vents blowing air that is barely cooler than the room
  • An AC that turns on and off every few minutes but never cools down the house
  • A burning smell or repeated breaker trips when the AC starts
  • Clicking or humming outside with no fan movement
  • Cooling bills climbing while the house stays sticky and warm
  • An iced-over evaporator coil or frozen refrigerant lines
  • A hiss or gurgle from the lines, a common sign of leaking refrigerant

Why Professional AC Repair Matters

Air conditioning repair concentrates several hazards in one cabinet. Capacitors store a charge that bites long after the breaker is off, condensing units run on 240-volt circuits, and the refrigerant loop is a sealed, pressurized system that federal rules do not permit venting to the air; working on it takes gauges, a recovery machine, and a vacuum pump, not a can from the internet. The diagnostic traps are just as real: a frozen coil looks like a refrigerant problem but is just as often an airflow problem, and refilling a leaking system without repairing the leak wastes money while acid and moisture quietly damage the compressor.

Professional repair gets the whole picture. Our experienced technicians meter electrical components instead of swapping them, weigh charge decisions against measured superheat and subcooling, find leaks with detectors and dye rather than assumptions, and check the filter, blower, and duct static pressure whenever airflow is suspect. That is the difference between a repair that holds through the season and a parts-cannon fix that calls you back in three weeks. And when the system genuinely is not worth saving, we tell you that plainly too, with numbers instead of pressure.

How to Prevent AC Repair Problems

A little routine maintenance goes a long way toward avoiding a mid-season breakdown. Follow these tips from our experienced technicians:

  • Leave vents open in every room, since closing them strains the blower
  • Have the AC serviced every spring, ahead of the first real heat
  • A new buzz or click at startup often means a weak capacitor, so call before it fails
  • Keep shrubs two feet from the outdoor unit and gently rinse its coil each spring
  • Shade is fine, but do not box in the outdoor unit with fences or covers
  • Clear the condensate drain when you first turn on the AC each year
  • Replace the filter every month in summer, since restricted airflow is the top cause of frozen coils
